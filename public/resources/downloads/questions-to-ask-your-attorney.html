Questions to Ask Your Estate Planning Attorney

Bring this to your first meeting. The goal isn’t to interrogate — it’s to understand exactly what you’re buying, what happens after signing, and how your attorney will work with the rest of your professional team. General preparation guidance only; once engaged, your attorney’s advice governs.

Fit and focus

  • What share of your practice is estate planning?
  • Do you regularly work with families like ours (asset level, business ownership, blended family, special circumstances)?
  • Are you licensed in our state, and experienced with issues in states where we own property?
  • Who in your office will we actually work with — and who answers questions after we sign?

Scope and process

  • Exactly which documents does the engagement include — and what is explicitly not included?
  • How is trust funding handled? Do you retitle assets, provide instructions for us to execute, or neither?
  • Who confirms that funding actually got completed?
  • How do you coordinate with our CPA and financial advisor, and at what points?
  • What's your typical timeline from engagement to signing?
  • How are future updates handled — amendments, restatements, periodic reviews — and what do they cost?
Why the funding questions matter: unfunded trusts are the most common way good documents fail. There is no wrong answer to “who does the retitling” — but there is a wrong outcome: everyone assuming someone else is doing it.

Cost

  • Is the fee flat or hourly, and what does the quote cover?
  • What commonly causes fees to exceed the quote?
  • What will a future amendment or restatement typically cost?

What to bring

  • Your asset inventory with values and titling (see our Document Inventory Worksheet).
  • Current beneficiary designations for retirement accounts and life insurance.
  • Any existing estate planning documents, even outdated ones.
  • Your people list: beneficiaries, plus candidates (and backups) for executor, trustee, agents, and guardians.
  • Your questions from this guide, with the three that matter most to you marked.

One signal to watch for

In the first meeting, notice whether the attorney asks more questions than they answer. Good planning is fact-driven — an attorney probing about family dynamics and long-term intentions is doing the job well.
