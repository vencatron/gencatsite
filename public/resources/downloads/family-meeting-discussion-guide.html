Family Meeting Discussion Guide

Research on failed wealth transfers points to one cause above all others: families that never talked. This guide structures the conversation most families postpone. It is general guidance for any family — adapt freely, and leave legal questions for a licensed attorney.

Before the meeting

  • Decide who attends. Common pattern: the parents/plan-makers plus adult children. Spouses of children and minor children are judgment calls — consistency matters more than the choice.
  • Set expectations in the invitation. “We want to talk about our plans so nobody is ever surprised” lands better than a mysterious summons.
  • Agree what is on and off the table. Many families discuss roles and intentions without disclosing dollar amounts. That is a valid choice — say it out loud at the start.
  • Pick neutral ground and a time limit. Ninety minutes is plenty for a first meeting.
Ground rules that work: one person speaks at a time; questions are welcome, arguments are deferred; nothing is decided in the meeting itself; what’s shared stays in the family.

Agenda 1 — Why we’re doing this (10 min)

  • What we want this family to be able to say about how we handled things, years from now.
  • What we’ve seen other families get wrong, and what we want to avoid.

Agenda 2 — The plan, at altitude (20 min)

  • Whether a plan exists, what kind (will, trust, both), and roughly when it was last updated.
  • Where documents are kept and who the professionals are (attorney, CPA, advisor) — names and contact information.
  • What the plan is trying to accomplish, in plain words.

Agenda 3 — The roles (25 min)

  • Who has been named for each job — executor, trustee, financial agent, health care agent, guardian — and why.
  • What each job actually involves. (Many people discover at this point that “trustee” is work, not an honor.)
  • Does each named person accept? Reluctance discovered now is a gift; reluctance discovered at a crisis is a disaster.

Agenda 4 — Wishes and the hard topics (25 min)

  • Health care wishes and end-of-life preferences — said out loud, so agents never have to guess.
  • The house, the business, the heirlooms: anything where “who gets it” carries emotion beyond money.
  • Anything unequal in the plan, and the reasoning. Unexplained surprises are the seed of most estate conflict.

Agenda 5 — Questions and next steps (10 min)

  • Open floor for questions (park anything contentious for a follow-up).
  • Agree on the follow-ups: documents to update with the attorney, designations to check, and when the family will talk again. Once a year is a good rhythm.

Questions that open people up

  • “What would you want to know if something happened to us tomorrow?”
  • “Is there anything you’d dread having to figure out on your own?”
  • “Which of these jobs would you genuinely not want?”
  • “What does a fair outcome look like to you — and does fair mean equal?”
