The Family Estate Planning Readiness Checklist

Work through this checklist before you meet with any professional. A prepared family gets a better plan, at a better price, from every advisor it engages. This is general preparation guidance — not legal, tax, or financial advice.

1. The people

  • List everyone who depends on you: spouse or partner, children (note any minors), parents, and anyone else you support.
  • Note family circumstances that matter to planning: prior marriages, blended family relationships, a loved one with special needs, family conflict.
  • Brainstorm candidates for the key roles — executor, trustee, financial agent, health care agent, and guardian for minor children — with at least one backup each.
  • For each candidate, ask: are they willing, able, organized, and likely to be available when needed?

2. The assets

  • Inventory what you own: real estate, bank and brokerage accounts, retirement accounts, business interests, life insurance, vehicles, and significant personal property.
  • For each asset, record the approximate value and — importantly — how it is titled (individual, joint, community property, entity, trust).
  • Pull the current beneficiary designations for every retirement account, life insurance policy, and annuity. Do not rely on memory.
  • List your debts: mortgages, loans, and any guarantees you have signed.
  • Use our companion Document Inventory Worksheet to capture this in one place.

3. The documents you already have

  • Gather any existing estate planning documents — wills, trusts, powers of attorney, health care directives — even if outdated.
  • Note when each was signed and in which state.
  • If you have a trust: list which assets, if any, were actually retitled into it. (This is the most commonly unfinished step in all of estate planning.)
  • Identify where original documents are physically kept, and who knows.

4. The decisions to think about

  • Who should receive what — and are there reasons for anything other than equal shares?
  • Should anyone receive their inheritance over time or with conditions, rather than all at once?
  • Who should make financial and medical decisions for you if you cannot?
  • What are your wishes for end-of-life care?
  • Are there charitable intentions to build into the plan?
  • If you own a business: who could run it, buy it, or wind it down — and does anyone know the plan?

5. Next steps

  • Decide which professionals to engage. Legal documents require a licensed estate planning attorney; the tax and coordination side is CPA territory.
  • Prepare your questions — see our companion guide, “Questions to Ask Your Attorney.”
  • Schedule the family conversation — see our Family Meeting Discussion Guide.
  • Put a yearly reminder on the calendar to review beneficiaries, titles, and life changes.
